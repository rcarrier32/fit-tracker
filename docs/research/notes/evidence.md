# Scientific evidence base for resistance-training program design rules

Scope note: these notes cover the evidence for a plan generator (goals: strength, muscle, fat_loss, athletic, durability) and an adaptive layer driven by logged weights and reps. Search budget was limited to about 18 calls. Two full-text hosts (sportrxiv.org, preprints.org) were blocked by the session network, so several exact coefficients come from abstracts or reliable secondary summaries and are marked as such. Items the search did not reach are listed under Gaps with suggested citations to check. They are not presented as findings.

## Weekly sets per muscle: dose-response, fractional counting, diminishing returns, training status

### Takeaway
More weekly sets produce more hypertrophy and strength, with diminishing returns. Returns flatten much sooner for strength than for muscle size. Indirect sets are best counted as 0.5 of a set ("fractional" counting). At a fixed weekly volume, frequency does little for hypertrophy but clearly helps strength.

### Cited Findings
- Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017 (J Sports Sci; 15 studies, 34 treatment groups): weekly sets treated as a continuous variable had a significant effect on muscle growth (P = 0.002). The categorical analysis (<5, 5-9, 10+ sets/muscle/week) showed only a trend (P = 0.074). The data could not show whether more than 10 weekly sets add benefit. Secondary sources summarize this as "graded increase up to 10+ sets." — [Schoenfeld et al. 2017 PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/Brad-Schoenfeld/publication/305455324_Dose-response_relationship_between_weekly_resistance_training_volume_and_increases_in_muscle_mass_A_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis/links/59dc0269458515e9ab4527d6/Dose-response-relationship-between-weekly-resistance-training-volume-and-increases-in-muscle-mass-A-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis.pdf)
- Pelland, Remmert, Robinson, Hinson & Zourdos: "The Resistance Training Dose Response: Meta-Regressions Exploring the Effects of Weekly Volume and Frequency on Muscle Hypertrophy and Strength Gains." Preprint SportRxiv 2024 (revised March 2025), published in Sports Medicine 2026;56(2):481-505. It pooled 67 studies (~2,058 participants). — [Sponet record](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)
- Pelland: each set was classified as direct or indirect to the measured muscle or lift. Indirect sets were weighted 1 ("total"), 0.5 ("fractional") or 0 ("direct only"). The fractional method had the strongest relative evidence and was used for the primary models. — [Sponet record](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en); [Abertay / summary](https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-the-dose-response-relationship-between-estimated-resist/)
- Pelland: the posterior probability that the volume slope is above zero was 100% for both hypertrophy and strength. Strength showed more pronounced diminishing returns than hypertrophy. — [Sponet record](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)
- Pelland: once volume is accounted for, frequency has a negligible, hard-to-identify effect on hypertrophy. Strength gains increased with frequency (posterior probability 100%), and this held after adjusting for volume and training status. — [Sponet record](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en); [Train.fitness summary](https://train.fitness/personal-trainer-blogs/not-all-sets-are-created-equal)
- A 2026 non-peer-reviewed preprint reanalysing Pelland's data (testing Beardsley's "weekly net stimulus" model) uses a hypertrophy subset of 220 effects from 35 studies. It agrees that volume shows diminishing returns and frequency has limited effect at matched volume. — [Preprints.org 202610.1022](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202610.1022)

### Inferences
- Rule for the plan generator: count a compound lift as 1 set for its prime movers and 0.5 for synergists (for example, bench press = 1 chest, 0.5 triceps, 0.5 front delt). This matches the best-supported counting method.
- Because of diminishing returns, a moderate default (about 10-20 fractional sets/muscle/week for the muscle goal) captures most of the benefit. For the strength goal, fewer sets on the target lift spread over more days (about 2-3 exposures per week) fits the evidence better than piling on volume.
- The adaptive layer should add volume slowly (for example, +1-2 sets/muscle/week per block) only while performance keeps improving, because each extra set buys less.

### Gaps
- Exact marginal-gain-per-set numbers and the volume where returns flatten in Pelland (the full text was unreachable because sportrxiv.org was blocked). The commonly quoted "0.37% growth per set" figure for Schoenfeld 2017 could not be verified.
- Baz-Valle et al. 2022 (PMC8884877, "A Systematic Review of the Effects of Different Resistance Training Volumes on Muscle Hypertrophy") appeared in results but was not read. It reportedly finds trained subjects benefit from 12-20 sets/week, with no clear advantage above ~20. Verify before use: [PMC8884877](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8884877).
- Beginner vs trained moderation: Pelland adjusted for training status, but the search did not reveal a separate beginner dose curve.

## Proximity to failure (RIR) and RPE-based autoregulation vs fixed loading

### Takeaway
Hypertrophy improves as sets end closer to failure. Strength gains are roughly flat across a wide range of RIR. Autoregulation (RPE, APRE, velocity) is at least as good as fixed percentage loading for strength, and some pooled data favor it in athletes.

### Cited Findings
- Robinson et al. 2024, Sports Medicine (DOI 10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2): hypertrophy improved as sets ended closer to failure. Strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR. The authors call the analysis exploratory, and RIR was estimated rather than measured. — [FAU press release](https://www.fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/muscle-growth-strength-study); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20240801/New-study-reveals-sweet-spot-for-muscle-growth.aspx)
- Press coverage of Robinson 2024 suggests 0-2 RIR for muscle growth, and heavier loads rather than failure for strength (an interpretation, not a coefficient). — [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20240801/New-study-reveals-sweet-spot-for-muscle-growth.aspx); [Biolayne review](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-14/the-controversy-of-training-to-failure-hypertrophy-versus-strength/)
- Zhang et al. 2021, Frontiers in Physiology (8 studies, 166 athletes, 2010-2020): autoregulation beat fixed loading for maximal strength (ES = 0.64, P < 0.001, I² = 0%). APRE was the most effective variant, and benefits appeared within blocks of 8 weeks or less. — [Zhang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.651112)
- Hickmott et al. 2022, Sports Medicine - Open (resistance-trained adults): autoregulated vs standardized load gave no significant 1RM difference. Velocity-loss thresholds ≤25% gave greater 1RM gains than >25%. Thresholds >20-25% were better for hypertrophy because they accumulate more volume. — [Hickmott et al. 2022 PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9.pdf); [PubMed 35038063](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35038063/)
- Zhang and Hickmott conflict: Zhang (athletes, smaller pool) favors autoregulation, while Hickmott (trained adults, load-only comparison) finds parity. — [Zhang](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.651112); [Hickmott](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35038063/)

### Inferences
- Goal-specific RIR defaults: muscle/fat_loss at 0-3 RIR (closer to failure on isolation and machine work), strength at 1-4 RIR on heavy compounds, athletic/power work far from failure (velocity loss under ~20-25%).
- The adaptive layer can safely use logged reps plus an RIR/RPE rating to set the next load (APRE-style). The evidence says it is not worse than fixed percentages and may be better, which justifies building adjustments from logs.
- Training closer to failure makes RIR self-reports more accurate (Robinson, via press release), so occasional AMRAP/top sets can calibrate the user's RIR estimates.

### Gaps
- Refalo et al. (2022/2023 meta-analysis and 2024 RIR trials) were not retrieved. Helms et al. (RPE vs %1RM, 2018) and Graham & Cleather (2019, autoregulation by RPE in strength training) were not retrieved. Verify their numbers before citing.

## Rest intervals: hypertrophy, strength, fat loss

### Takeaway
Rest longer than 60 s gives a small hypertrophy benefit. No appreciable extra benefit appeared above ~90 s in the Singer meta-analysis. Short rest has no clear advantage for muscle and was not shown to matter meaningfully for fat loss in what was retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Singer et al. 2024, "Give it a rest," Frontiers in Sports and Active Living 6:1429789 (Bayesian meta-analysis of RCTs): a small hypertrophy benefit for rest >60 s, possibly through less volume-load drop-off. No appreciable difference when resting >90 s. Arm and thigh estimates leaned toward longer rest, while whole-body estimates were near zero (slightly favoring shorter rest). Heterogeneity was substantial, and training to failure or not did not change the rest effect. — [Singer et al. 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/abstract)
- The earlier SportRxiv preprint of Singer framed effects beyond 90 s as "unclear," so cite the peer-reviewed version. — [Frontiers full text](https://frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/text)

### Inferences
- Default rest: about 90-180 s for compounds and 60-90 s for isolation work. Longer rest (2-5 min) for heavy strength sets is reasonable, because the mechanism Singer proposes (keeping volume load) matters even more at high loads. Schoenfeld 2016 was not retrieved to confirm this.
- For fat_loss, keep rest lengths close to the hypertrophy defaults and rely on supersets of non-competing muscles (antagonist pairing) for time efficiency, not on very short rest.

### Gaps
- Schoenfeld et al. 2016 (3 min vs 1 min rest, JSCR) was not retrieved. It is widely cited as showing greater strength and hypertrophy with 3 min rest; verify.
- No source was found quantifying extra calorie burn (EPOC) from short-rest resistance training. Fat-loss rest guidance is unsupported by retrieved data.

## Progression methods, periodization, exercise variation

### Takeaway
Load progression and rep progression produce similar results in trained lifters. Rep progression slightly favored hypertrophy at one site, and load progression slightly favored 1RM. Moderate, planned exercise variation can help regional hypertrophy and strength. Excessive random rotation may hurt.

### Cited Findings
- Plotkin et al. 2022, PeerJ 10:e14142 (43 trained adults, 8 weeks, lower body): the LOAD group (more weight, fixed reps) and the REPS group (fixed weight, more reps) had similar outcomes. Rectus femoris growth modestly favored REPS and dynamic strength slightly favored LOAD, differences of questionable practical significance. — [Plotkin et al. 2022](https://peerj.com/articles/14142); [PubMed 36199287](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36199287/)
- Plotkin limitations: both groups started at 8-12 reps, the REPS group's sets to failure were harder to sustain, and subjects were young trained adults. — [Stanford Lifestyle Medicine](https://lifestylemedicine.stanford.edu/increasing-weight-or-increasing-reps-can-both-make-you-stronger/)
- Kassiano et al. 2022, JSCR 36(6):1753-1762 (8 studies, N = 241 young men): some systematic variation seems to enhance regional hypertrophy and maximize dynamic strength. Excessive, random variation, or variations that give a redundant stimulus, may compromise gains. — [PubMed 35438660](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/); [Biolayne commentary](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-7/exercise-variation-for-strength-and-hypertrophy-change-is-not-always-good/)

### Inferences
- Use double progression: add reps within a range until the top is hit at the target RIR, then add load and reset to the bottom of the range. Plotkin shows both levers work, and this combines them. Use load-first progression for strength-goal compound lifts (load progression slightly favored 1RM).
- Keep core lifts stable for at least a mesocycle (about 4-8 weeks) and swap accessories with a purpose (different region or angle, for example incline vs flat), not at random. Kassiano gives no exact interval, so the 4-8 week window is a design choice, not a finding.

### Gaps
- Linear vs undulating periodization meta-analyses (for example Moesgaard et al. 2022, Sports Med; Grgic et al. 2017) were not retrieved. No sourced effect size here.
- No study pinned an optimal exercise-change frequency.

## Deloads: frequency, scheduled vs autoregulated

### Takeaway
Deload evidence is thin and mostly expert opinion. The only controlled trial found that a full 1-week training stop at week 5 of 9 did not change hypertrophy but slightly reduced strength gains. Expert consensus treats deloads as a planned reduction in training stress, not a full stop.

### Cited Findings
- Coleman et al. 2024, PeerJ 12:e16777 (39 trained adults, 9 weeks): a 1-week complete training stop at the midpoint (DELOAD) vs continuous training (TRAD). No appreciable differences in lower-body muscle size, local endurance or power. TRAD gained more isometric and dynamic strength (lab blog: +4.5 kg squat 1RM and +11.5 Nm isometric knee extension favoring TRAD). — [Coleman et al. 2024](https://peerj.com/articles/16777); [PubMed 38274324](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/); [Lab blog](https://lookgreatnaked.com/blog/are-deloads-useless)
- Coleman caveat: only complete cessation was tested, not reduced volume or intensity. It was scheduled at week 5 whether or not participants needed it. — [Lab blog](https://lookgreatnaked.com/blog/are-deloads-useless)
- Bell et al. 2023, Sports Medicine - Open 9:87: a three-round Delphi of expert coaches (34 → 29 → 21) building consensus principles for integrating deloads into strength and physique programs. The authors state deloading is widely used but poorly researched. — [PMC10511399](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10511399/); [SHURA PDF](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/32417/1/s40798-023-00633-0.pdf)
- Related work by the same group: a 2022 interview study of 18 coaches, a 2024 survey of athlete deloading practices, and a 2025 Strength and Conditioning Journal practical-recommendations article. — [SHURA 35313](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/35313/); [Frontiers 2022 DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/f1457672bb654bc3b363b5caa64815b3)

### Inferences
- The default deload should reduce volume (fewer sets, more RIR), not stop training, because full stops slightly cost strength in Coleman.
- An autoregulated trigger (stalled or falling e1RM or reps across 2+ sessions, or high reported fatigue) is easier to justify than a fixed calendar, because no evidence favors a specific frequency. A fallback schedule of every ~4-8 weeks follows common practice but is unverified here.

### Gaps
- The specific Delphi consensus statements (frequency, duration, volume and intensity reductions) were not extracted. The PMC full text is available at the link above and should be read before citing figures such as "every 4-6 weeks" or "a 30-50% volume reduction."

## Warm-ups, core placement, exercise order

### Takeaway
Exercise order affects strength mainly through specificity: whatever lift is done first improves most. Order does not detectably affect hypertrophy. Retrieved evidence on warm-up protocols and core placement is insufficient.

### Cited Findings
- Nunes et al. 2021, European Journal of Sport Science (11 studies): no overall strength difference by order (ES = -0.11, p = 0.306). Multi-joint lifts gained more strength when done first (ES = 0.32, p = 0.034). Single-joint lifts gained more when done first (ES = -0.58, p = 0.032). No effect on hypertrophy (ES = 0.03, p = 0.862). — [PubMed 32077380](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32077380/)
- Limitation: most included studies measured hypertrophy only in the single-joint agonist, which limits the conclusion. — [Examine](https://examine.com/conditions/muscle-size-and-strength/faq/which-exercise-should-be-performed-first-in-a-workout)

### Inferences
- Put the goal-priority lift first: the main compound lift for strength/athletic, a lagging muscle's lift for muscle. Order is a free choice for hypertrophy.
- By the same logic, heavy core work (which stabilizes squats and deadlifts) is better placed after the main lifts. This is an inference from order specificity, not a direct finding.

### Gaps
- No sources retrieved on general vs specific warm-up effects or the number of ramp-up sets (for example Ribeiro et al. 2014/2020 on specific warm-up, and Fradkin 2010 meta-analysis on warm-up and performance). Not verified.
- No retrieved evidence on core "activation" or pre-fatigue from core work before lifting.

## Plyometric dosing, tendon/isometric and injury prevention, concurrent training

### Takeaway
Programs with the Nordic hamstring exercise roughly halve hamstring injury rates in pooled data, though a later reappraisal calls the RCT evidence inconclusive. The Copenhagen adductor program cut groin problems by 41% in an RCT. Concurrent aerobic training does not meaningfully hurt hypertrophy or maximal strength, but it blunts explosive strength, mostly when done in the same session.

### Cited Findings
- van Dyk et al. 2019, BJSM (15 studies, 8,459 athletes): programs including the Nordic hamstring exercise (NHE) gave a hamstring-injury risk ratio of 0.49 (95% CI 0.32-0.74), up to 51% fewer injuries. NHE could not be isolated from the other exercises in the programs. — [Physio-network summary](https://www.physio-network.com/research-reviews/thigh/including-the-nordic-hamstring-exercise-in-injury-prevention-programmes-halves-the-rate-of-hamstring-injuries-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-8459-athletes/); [QxMD](https://read.qxmd.com/read/30808663/including-the-nordic-hamstring-exercise-in-injury-prevention-programmes-halves-the-rate-of-hamstring-injuries-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-8459-athletes)
- A 2021 reappraisal (J Clin Epidemiol) found the evidence inconclusive and largely high risk of bias. Pooled across 5 RCTs, RR = 0.56 (95% CI 0.20-1.52). — [Physio-network reappraisal](https://physio-network.com/research-reviews/thigh/why-methods-matter-in-a-meta-analysis-a-reappraisal-showed-inconclusive-injury-preventive-effect-of-nordic-hamstring-exercise)
- Harøy et al. 2019, BJSM (DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2017-098937; cluster RCT, 35 teams, 652 male semiprofessional footballers): the Adductor Strengthening Programme (one Copenhagen adduction exercise, 3 progression levels) was done 3×/week in a 6-8 week preseason, then 1×/week for a 28-week season. It lowered the odds of groin problems by 41% (OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.40-0.86). Average season prevalence was 13.5% vs 21.3%. — [PMC6851742](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6851742); [ISRCTN98514933](https://www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN98514933)
- Players completed about 70% of prescribed sessions. In a 2024 survey, 84% of teams used the Copenhagen exercise but 65% had modified it. — [PMC11344511](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11344511)
- de Villarreal et al. 2009 meta-analysis (56 studies): vertical jump gains were most likely with >10 weeks, >20 sessions and high-intensity programs of >50 jumps per session. — [PubMed 19197203](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19197203/)
- A 2025 meta-analysis found low total ground-contact programs (<900 contacts) significantly improved vertical jump. A 2024 adult study using 100 contacts/session, 2×/week for 8 weeks, found no superiority of vertical, horizontal or combined jump programs (reported via a coaching article). — [Frontiers Physiol 2025 1639477](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1639477/xml); [Coach Athletics summary](https://coachathletics.com.au/coaching-education/plyometrics-not-just-foot-contacts)
- Plyometric dose is reported inconsistently across studies (jumps, contacts, sessions, sets×reps). — [Coach Athletics](https://coachathletics.com.au/coaching-education/plyometrics-not-just-foot-contacts)
- Schumann et al. 2022, Sports Medicine (43 studies): concurrent vs strength-only training effects were maximal strength SMD -0.06 (NS), explosive strength -0.28 (p = 0.007), hypertrophy -0.01 (NS). Explosive-strength loss was larger when both were done in the same session than when separated by ≥3 h. Results did not depend on aerobic type, frequency, training status or age. — [Schumann et al. 2022 PDF](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/71546/1/Schumann2021_Article_CompatibilityOfConcurrentAerob.pdf)
- Older and fiber-level contrasts: Wilson et al. 2012 found running (not cycling) concurrent with lifting reduced hypertrophy and strength. Lundberg et al. 2022 found a small fiber-hypertrophy cost (SMD -0.23), largest for type I fibers with running (-0.81). — [Schumann PDF (discussion)](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/71546/1/Schumann2021_Article_CompatibilityOfConcurrentAerob.pdf); [Concurrent training meta-analysis record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_1027372546)

### Inferences
- Durability goal: include an NHE progression (or another eccentric hamstring exercise) and a Copenhagen adduction progression. The Harøy dosing (3×/week to build up, then 1×/week maintenance) is a ready-made template.
- Athletic goal: about 40-100 contacts per session, 1-3×/week, done fresh (before lifting), with total program contacts in the hundreds. Separate hard cardio from plyometric and power sessions by ≥3 h, or put it on other days.
- Fat_loss goal: adding cardio does not cost muscle or maximal strength on average. Prefer cycling or low-impact cardio when volume is high, given the running signal in older and fiber-level data.

### Gaps
- No sourced data was retrieved on isometric or tendon loading protocols (for example, isometric holds for patellar or Achilles tendinopathy) or on general durability dosing beyond NHE and Copenhagen.
- Plyometric contact thresholds for beginners vs advanced (the commonly cited NSCA 80-100 / 100-120 / 120-140 contacts per session table) were not verified.

## Core and glute training volume

### Takeaway
No direct dose-response evidence for core or glute volume was retrieved. Apply the general volume findings (fractional counting, 10+ sets with diminishing returns) and count indirect work from squats, hinges and lunges at 0.5.

### Cited Findings
- The fractional (0.5) counting of indirect sets from Pelland applies to any muscle, including glutes stimulated by squats and hip hinges. — [Sponet record](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)

### Inferences
- Glutes already get a lot of fractional volume from lower-body compounds. Direct glute work (hip thrust, abduction) may only be needed to reach the target for muscle or glute-priority goals.
- Core: 2-3 short direct sets per session after main lifts is a reasonable design choice, unsupported by retrieved dose data.

### Gaps
- No retrieved meta-analyses on core training volume, glute-specific dose-response, or "glute activation" warm-ups. Plotkin 2023 (hip thrust vs squat) and Barbalho / Neto glute EMG work were not searched.
- No ACSM 2026 position stand update was found or searched for. The ACSM 2009 progression models and NSCA guidance were not retrieved.
