# Evidence for short daily / frequent mobility, joint-health and injury-prevention work for a ~40-year-old basketball player-coach

Scope note: about 21 searches. Most figures come from abstracts, PubMed/PMC records or indexed summaries, not full texts. Figures that were reported secondhand are marked. Most trials studied youth, adolescent or elite players; direct RCT evidence in recreational/masters basketball players aged 35-45 is almost nonexistent, so application to this person is inference.

## 1. Ankle sprain prevention: balance/proprioceptive training, dose; dorsiflexion and knee

### Takeaway
Balance training is the best-supported item for a basketball player. It cuts ankle sprain risk by about 35-46%, and the effect is largest in people with a previous sprain (risk roughly halved). Protocols ran 3-5x/week, about 10-30 min, for 4-8+ weeks, so it fits a short daily routine. Low dorsiflexion range was a prospective risk factor for jumper's knee in one basketball cohort, but the wider evidence on that is conflicting.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis of RCTs in athletes (n=3,577): balance training reduced sprain risk by 46% (RR 0.54). Bracing reduced it by 64% in the same review. — [Bellows & Wong 2018, Int J Sports Phys Ther (PMC6044595)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6044595)
- Meta-analysis of 8 trials: pooled RR 0.62 (95% CI 0.43-0.90), about a 38% reduction. — [de Vasconcelos et al. 2018, Clin Rehabil (PubMed 30038824)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30038824/)
- Soccer: programmes that included balance exercises cut ankle injury rate by about 36% per 1,000 h. Balance exercises alone: IRR 0.58 (95% CI 0.41-0.84). — [Al Attar et al. 2022, J Physiother, via Physio-Network](https://www.physio-network.com/research-reviews/ankle-foot/injury-prevention-programs-that-include-balance-training-exercises-reduce-ankle-injury-rates-among-soccer-players-a-systematic-review/)
- McGuine & Keene 2006 (AJSM 34(7):1103-11), cluster RCT, 765 high-school soccer and basketball players: 1.13 vs 1.87 sprains per 1,000 exposures (p=0.04). With a prior sprain, RR 0.56. Without a prior sprain the difference was not significant (4.3% vs 7.7%, p=0.059). The programme progressed from eyes-open floor work to eyes-closed balance board. Not blinded. — [AAFP summary](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2006/1115/p1777.html); [QxMD/PubMed 16476915](https://read.qxmd.com/read/16476915/the-effect-of-a-balance-training-program-on-the-risk-of-ankle-sprains-in-high-school-athletes)
- Hupperets et al. 2009 (BMJ 339:b2684), RCT: an unsupervised 8-week home proprioceptive programme added to usual care after a sprain cut recurrence by 35% (RR 0.63, 95% CI 0.45-0.88). NNT ≈ 9. The benefit was concentrated in sprains that had not been medically treated. — [PubMed 19589822](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19589822/); [PMC2714677](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2714677)
- An older systematic review found a larger preventive effect in people with a previous sprain. — [McKeon & Hertel 2008 (PubMed 18523567)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18523567/)
- Neuromuscular-training dose: Emery 2015 meta-analysis (youth, 25 studies): lower-extremity injury IRR 0.64 (0.49-0.84). Knee IRR 0.74 (0.51-1.07, not significant). Steib 2017 dose-response meta-analysis (16 trials): IRR 0.58 overall. Training 2x/week gave IRR 0.50 and 3x/week gave IRR 0.40, the largest reductions. Short, frequent sessions did best. — [Emery 2015, BJSM (PubMed 26084526)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26084526/); [Steib 2017, Front Physiol (PMC5694483)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5694483/)
- Dorsiflexion: Backman & Danielson 2011 (AJSM), 1-year prospective study of 90 junior elite basketball players. Players who developed patellar tendinopathy had about 4.7-5.1° less dorsiflexion at baseline. Suggested screening cutoff 36.5°. Proposed mechanism: the patellar tendon takes over load the ankle cannot. — [PubMed 21917610](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21917610/)
- A 2018 BJSM systematic review rated the evidence for low dorsiflexion as a patellar tendinopathy risk factor as limited/conflicting. — [Modifiable risk factors for PT, PubMed 30054341](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30054341/)

### Inferences
- A daily 3-5 min single-leg balance block (eyes closed, unstable surface, perturbations) is justified. The trial doses were 3-5x/week, so daily practice is in range and builds habit. It should be a priority for anyone with a previous sprain; sprain history is very common in basketball.
- A short daily dorsiflexion mobilisation (knee-to-wall lunge) is cheap and biologically plausible for knee load, but its injury-prevention support rests on one cohort. It is a low-cost "maybe" rather than an evidence-mandated item.

### Gaps
- No balance-training RCT specific to adults over 35 in basketball was found.
- No RCT was found showing that increasing dorsiflexion reduces knee injury.
- No head-to-head comparison of daily versus 3x/week balance work was found.

## 2. Knee: patellar tendinopathy (isometrics, HSR, Spanish squat) and basketball neuromuscular warm-ups

### Takeaway
For patellar tendon health the evidence favours progressive heavy/slow loading 2-3x/week, not daily work. Isometrics give short-term pain relief (up to about 45 min) but are not better than isotonic loading over weeks. FIFA 11+ cut overall injuries in elite basketball, but not knee or ankle injuries specifically.

### Cited Findings
- Kongsgaard et al. 2009 (Scand J Med Sci Sports), RCT with 39 men over 12 weeks: corticosteroid vs eccentric decline squat vs heavy slow resistance (HSR). All improved on VISA-P at 12 weeks. Eccentric and HSR gains held at 6 months; corticosteroid gains deteriorated. HSR had the highest satisfaction at 6 months and showed improvement in pathology and collagen turnover. — [PubMed 19793213](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19793213/)
- After 12 weeks of HSR, clinical improvement was associated with tendon fibril morphology normalising (n=8). — [Kongsgaard 2010, AJSM (QxMD 20154324)](https://read.qxmd.com/read/20154324/fibril-morphology-and-tendon-mechanical-properties-in-patellar-tendinopathy-effects-of-heavy-slow-resistance-training)
- Network meta-analysis 2021: isometrics were about as effective as isotonics for immediate pain relief (3 RCTs). Eccentric loading with or without adjuncts was recommended as first line. — [BMJ Open SEM 2021 (PMC8634001)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8634001/)
- Clifford et al. 2020 meta-analysis (10 RCTs across tendinopathies, 4 of them patellar): isometrics were not superior to isotonics either immediately or in the short term (≤12 weeks). Evidence level 3. — [BMJ Open SEM 2020 (PubMed 32818059)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32818059/)
- In-season athletes: isometrics gave greater immediate relief, up to 45 min after the session. Both methods reduced pain over 4 weeks. — [Vang & Niznik 2021, J Sport Rehabil (summary in search results)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32818059/) *(the citation is secondhand; the original was not retrieved)*
- Longo et al. 2012 (AJSM 40:996-1005), cluster RCT in 121 elite male basketball players over 9 months: FIFA 11+ lowered overall, training, trunk, leg and hip/groin injury rates. It did not significantly reduce knee or ankle injuries. Some reviews give conflicting details (FIFA 11 vs 11+; ages 12-19). — [PEDro record](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/32191); [Geier commentary](https://drdavidgeier.com/injury-prevention-basketball-fifa11/amp/)

### Inferences
- Patellar tendon loading (Spanish squat, slow split squat or leg press at about 3-4 s up and 3-4 s down, heavy) belongs in the 2-3x/week slot. A 30-45 s wall-sit or Spanish-squat isometric (e.g. 5 x 45 s) before a game or practice is a reasonable *symptomatic* tool and could be done daily if the knee is cranky. It is not a proven preventive.
- An 11+-style warm-up before every practice or game is the realistic "daily" neuromuscular dose for a coach. The Steib dose data favour ≥2-3 exposures per week.

### Gaps
- No RCT evidence was found on Spanish squats specifically. Their use rests on clinical practice and biomechanics.
- No primary-prevention RCT of tendon loading for patellar tendinopathy in adult recreational players was found.

## 3. Achilles/calf: soleus, tendon aging, calf strain after 35

### Takeaway
Age is one of the two strongest risk factors for calf strain; the other is a previous strain. The soleus accounts for most calf strains. The aging Achilles still adapts, but it needs high-strain loading (about 80-90% MVC) for roughly 3-4 months. That is heavy calf work 2-3x/week; daily light heel raises do not provide it.

### Cited Findings
- Systematic review: chronological age and previous calf strain are the strongest risk factors for calf muscle injury. The evidence is sparse and at high risk of bias. — [Green & Pizzari 2017, BJSM 51:1189](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/51/16/1189.abstract)
- Calf strain incidence rises with age in UEFA data, unlike hamstring, quad and groin strains; players >30 are at higher risk. — [Aspetar Journal: calf injuries in track and field](https://journal.aspetar.com/en/journals/volume-13-targeted-topic-sports-medicine-in-athletics/calf-muscle-injuries-in-track-and-field-athletes); [FMPA "Under the surface"](https://www.fmpa.co.uk/app/uploads/2026/03/Under-the-surface.pdf)
- English FA data: 75% (251) of calf injuries involved the soleus. — [FMPA "Under the surface"](https://www.fmpa.co.uk/app/uploads/2026/03/Under-the-surface.pdf)
- Arampatzis 2007: only high-strain loading increased Achilles stiffness and modulus, which suggests a strain threshold. High-strain protocol: 4 reps at 90% MVC vs 7 reps at 55%. — [J Exp Biol 210:2743](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article/210/15/2743/17005/Adaptational-responses-of-the-human-Achilles)
- Women aged 60-75: a 14-week high-strain programme increased Achilles stiffness through both material and dimensional changes. Continued training maintained the gains but produced no further gains, so the response is nonlinear. — [Epro/Karamanidis et al. (PMC5835978)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5835978)
- A review suggests about 3-4 months of 80-90% MVC loading is needed to counter age-related loss of tendon stiffness, which comes mostly from material rather than size changes. — [core.ac.uk review](https://core.ac.uk/download/227105947.pdf) *(a recommendation, not a measured timeline)*
- Young men, 2.5-5 sessions/week with 180-300 s of total high-strain loading: frequency and volume mattered less than expected. — [Scientific Reports 2024 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/6de7373a65ab439ea4609085bb4a8f6e)

### Inferences
- Do heavy seated soleus raises and single-leg straight-knee raises (slow, with load) 2-3x/week. A daily low-load calf/ankle primer (e.g. 1 x 15-20 single-leg raises, bent-knee and straight) fits the warm-up and keeps capacity "visited", but does not replace the heavy sessions.
- For a ~40-year-old, calf/soleus capacity is arguably the highest-value tendon target, because of age-linked calf strain risk and the jumping and decelerating demands of basketball.

### Gaps
- Age-specific Achilles rupture incidence by decade was not retrieved.
- No RCT showing that calf strengthening prevents calf strains or Achilles ruptures was found.
- Eccentric heel-drop (Alfredson) studies are management evidence for tendinopathy; no prevention data were retrieved.

## 4. Hamstring and groin: Nordic and Copenhagen, minimal doses

### Takeaway
Programmes containing the Nordic hamstring exercise roughly halved hamstring injuries (RR 0.49), though a reappraisal questions how much of that is the Nordic itself. Low-volume Nordics (1-2 sets of 3-5 reps, 1-2x/week) still raise eccentric strength. Copenhagen adductions at very low in-season doses (1x/week, ≤1 set of 12-15 per side) cut groin problems by 41%. Both are 1-2x/week exercises, not daily ones.

### Cited Findings
- van Dyk, Behan & Whiteley 2019 (BJSM 53:1362), 15 studies, 8,459 athletes: injury risk ratio 0.49 (0.32-0.74). RCTs only: 0.52 (0.32-0.85). Low risk-of-bias studies only: 0.55 (0.34-0.89). — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/early/2019/02/26/bjsports-2018-100045.long)
- A reappraisal argued that study selection mixed in multi-exercise programmes and found the Nordic's preventive effect inconclusive. The revised figures were not retrieved. — [Physio-Network review of the reappraisal](https://physio-network.com/research-reviews/thigh/why-methods-matter-in-a-meta-analysis-a-reappraisal-showed-inconclusive-injury-preventive-effect-of-nordic-hamstring-exercise)
- Cuthbert 2019 meta-analysis: lower Nordic volume did not compromise eccentric strength or architecture adaptations versus high volume. — [Salford repository](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1489259)
- Cholp & Zemková 2025 meta-analysis (soccer): low volume (about 1-2 sets of 3-5 reps, 1-2x/week) improved eccentric strength (g=0.46, CI 0.06-0.87) but not fascicle length or architecture. High volume was better for architecture. The two reviews conflict on architecture. — [Front Physiol 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1631205/full)
- Harøy et al. 2019 (BJSM 53:145), cluster RCT with 652 male footballers: Copenhagen adduction 3x/week preseason, then 1x/week in season. Groin problem risk 41% lower (OR 0.59, 0.40-0.86). Season prevalence 13.5% vs 21.3%. The outcome was self-reported (OSTRC). — [ISRCTN98514933](https://www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN98514933); [PMC6851742](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6851742)
- Dose note: maximum 1 set of 12-15 reps per side; about 18% lower risk of a "substantial" groin problem. — [Therapeutic Associates commentary](https://www.therapeuticassociates.com/copenhagen-adductor-exercise/)

### Inferences
- Nordics at 2 x 3-5 reps, 1-2x/week, plus Copenhagens (short or long lever) at 1-2 x 6-15 per side, 1-2x/week, give near-maximal protection for minimal time. They are fatiguing eccentric exercises, so a daily slot is unnecessary and could cause soreness.
- Hamstring and groin strains are less dominant in basketball than in soccer, so these rank below ankle and calf work but remain cheap insurance.

### Gaps
- No basketball-specific Nordic or Copenhagen injury RCT was found.

## 5. Hips: mobility/strength, low back and knee pain, internal rotation

### Takeaway
Hip ROM, especially total rotation and internal rotation, is linked to groin pain in athletes. A link to low back pain is plausible but was not established by any review retrieved. Screening hip ROM predicts poorly.

### Cited Findings
- Total hip rotation (both hips) below 85° at preseason predicted groin pain. Earlier reviews conflict. — [Tak et al. 2018, BJSM (PubMed 29449206)](https://www.pubmed.gov/29449206/)
- Reduced internal rotation and bent-knee fall-out best separated athletes with and without hip/groin pain. — [PMC5754850](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5754850)
- Total hip ROM is the factor most consistently related to groin pain, but screening is unlikely to identify at-risk athletes because the differences are small and measurement properties are poor. — [BJSM 51:1611](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/51/22/1611)
- A systematic review on hip ROM and nonspecific low back pain exists, but its conclusions were not retrieved. — [Avman et al. 2019, Musculoskelet Sci Pract (scholarhub)](https://scholarhub.vn/publication/Is-there-an-association-between-hip-range-of-motion-and-nonspecific-low-back-pain-A-systematic-review/a7795d8e-5f0f-4851-803c-458beb2c587f)

### Inferences
- A daily hip rotation drill (90/90 transitions, active internal/external rotation lift-offs) is low cost and targets the variable most linked to hip/groin problems. Expect a modest, unproven preventive effect.
- Hip strength work (side planks, Copenhagens, split squats) fits the 2-3x/week strength sessions.

### Gaps
- No quantified hip IR–low back pain evidence in athletes was retrieved.
- No intervention trial was found showing that improving hip ROM prevents injury.

## 6. Shoulders: rotator cuff/scapular routines

### Takeaway
The best evidence is a warm-up programme in elite handball: the OSTRC Shoulder Injury Prevention Programme, done 3x/week as part of warm-up, reduced shoulder problems. Basketball is not an overhead-throwing sport, so the case for daily band work is weak for this person unless he has a shoulder history.

### Cited Findings
- Andersson et al. 2017 (BJSM 51:1073), cluster RCT with 660 elite handball players. The programme was done 3x/week in warm-up and targeted glenohumeral internal rotation ROM, external rotation strength and scapular control. It reduced the prevalence of shoulder problems. Baseline weekly prevalence was 28%, with 12% substantial. The exact effect estimate was not retrieved; the commonly quoted "28% reduction" could not be confirmed and seems to be a confusion with the baseline prevalence. — [BJSM full text PDF](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/51/14/1073.full.pdf); [OSTRC project page](https://klokavskade.no/en/projects/preventing-overuse-shoulder-injuries-among-throwing-athletes-a-cluster-randomised-controlled-trial-in-660-elite-handball-players)

### Inferences
- External-rotation band work 2-3x/week, or folded into warm-ups, is sufficient. Daily band work is optional and has no direct evidence behind it.

### Gaps
- No basketball shoulder-prevention RCT was found, and no evidence on daily versus 3x/week band work.

## 7. Stretching and mobility: dose-response, pre-play static stretching, age-related tissue changes

### Takeaway
Range-of-motion gains depend on *weekly accumulated* stretching time. About 5-10 min per muscle group per week is enough to gain ROM. Spread across 5+ days, that is roughly 1-2 min per muscle per day, which suits a daily routine. Static holds under 60 s per muscle inside a full warm-up cost only about 1-2% in strength and power. Holds of 60 s or more cost about 4-7.5%.

### Cited Findings
- Thomas et al. 2018 (Int J Sports Med), review of 23 articles: weekly time, not time per session, drove ROM gains. They suggested at least 5 min/week per muscle, spread over at least 5 days/week. — [PubMed 29506306](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29506306/)
- 2025 Sports Medicine dose meta-analysis: flexibility gains were maximised at about 4 min per session (acute) and 10 min/week (chronic). Weekly frequency did not moderate the effect, which conflicts with Thomas on frequency. — [Springer, s40279-024-02002-7](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s40279-024-02002-7); [PubMed 39614059](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39614059/)
- Acute pre-play effects: short static stretching (≤60 s per muscle) gives a trivial 1-2% loss in strength and power, especially within a full warm-up. Longer durations give 4.0-7.5% losses. Behm 2016 figures, reported secondhand: ≥60 s gives -4.6%, <60 s gives -1.1%, PNF -3.7%, dynamic stretching +1.3%. — [Chaabene et al. 2019, Front Physiol (PMC6895680)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6895680/)
- Age and tendon: tendon stiffness and modulus decline with age, mainly through material change, but aging tendons stay mechanosensitive (see section 3). — [core.ac.uk review](https://core.ac.uk/download/227105947.pdf); [PMC5835978](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5835978)

### Inferences
- For a daily routine, about 60-90 s total per target area (calves/soleus, hip flexors, hip rotators, hamstrings) reaches the 5-10 min/week threshold.
- Before games, use dynamic mobility plus short (<30-60 s) holds only for areas that need them. Put longer holds after play or at another time of day.
- Stretching changes ROM but has weak or absent evidence for preventing injury. It should be prioritised only where a ROM deficit is functionally relevant (e.g. dorsiflexion, hip rotation).

### Gaps
- No stretching data specific to ages 35-45 were found.
- Stretching's effect on injury incidence was not covered by the retrieved sources.

## 8. Minimal effective dose: retention of ROM and tendon gains

### Takeaway
ROM gains decline only partly over 2-6 weeks without stretching: still moderately above baseline (ES 0.55) but below the end-of-training level (ES -0.41). Ongoing low-frequency maintenance is needed. Tendon stiffness takes months to build in older adults, and continued loading maintains it. Injury-prevention exercises keep working at 1x/week in season (Copenhagen) and 1-2x/week (Nordic).

### Cited Findings
- Detraining meta-analysis 2025: 13 studies, 556 participants, 5-15 weeks of stretching then 2-6 weeks off. ROM was still above baseline (ES 0.55) but lower than at the end of training (ES -0.41). Weekly stretching frequency moderated retention. Only physically active participants kept gains through detraining. Certainty for maintenance was low. — [Sports Med Open 2025 (PMC12638565)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12638565/)
- Conflicting single study: after 5 weeks of static stretching followed by 5 weeks off, ROM returned to baseline. — [Front Physiol 2021, 656579](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.656579/full)
- Tendon: 14 weeks of high-strain loading increased stiffness in older adults; continued training maintained it without further gains. — [PMC5835978](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5835978)
- Maintenance doses: Copenhagen 1x/week in season ([ISRCTN98514933](https://www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN98514933)); Nordic at low volume, 1-2x/week, keeps eccentric strength gains ([Cholp & Zemková 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1631205/full)); neuromuscular training works best at 2-3x/week ([Steib 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5694483/)).

### Inferences
- Mobility gains need near-continuous exposure, which is why mobility suits the daily slot. Strength and tendon qualities hold at 1-2x/week once built, which is why they suit the 2-3x/week slot.
- A daily 10-12 min routine of 6-7 items: (1) single-leg balance/perturbation, (2) knee-to-wall dorsiflexion, (3) calf raises bent and straight knee (light), (4) hip 90/90 or rotation lift-offs, (5) hip flexor / couch stretch, (6) glute bridge or side plank (light activation), optionally (7) Spanish-squat or wall-sit isometric when the knee is symptomatic.
- 2-3x/week: heavy soleus/calf raises, HSR knee work or Spanish squats, Nordics, Copenhagens, rotator cuff external-rotation band work, plyometric landing drills (11+-style).

### Gaps
- No data were found on mobility maintenance beyond 6 weeks of detraining, or on the minimum maintenance frequency in masters athletes.

## 9. Recovery practices for masters athletes: evidence versus hype

### Takeaway
Foam rolling has small effects: about 6% less soreness (g=0.47) and a small flexibility gain. The authors say it is better justified as a warm-up tool than as a recovery tool. Short sleep (<8 h) was associated with about 1.7x injury risk in adolescents. That is plausible for adults but was not measured in them.

### Cited Findings
- Wiewelhove 2019 meta-analysis (21 studies): post-exercise soreness reduced 6% (g=0.47). Pre-exercise flexibility +4% (g=0.34), sprint +0.7% (g=0.28). Jump and strength effects negligible. The authors favour foam rolling as a warm-up tool. — [Front Physiol 2019 (PubMed 31024339)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31024339/)
- Critique: PainSci describes the sprint finding as driven mostly by one study and the evidence as weak. — [PainSci](https://www.painscience.com/biblio/foam-rolling-science--a-predictably-inconclusive-garbage-in-garbage-out-meta-analysis.html)
- Milewski et al. 2014, 112 athletes aged 12-18: <8 h sleep gave 1.7x injury risk (RR 1.7, 95% CI 1.0-3.0). Sleep was self-reported. Grade-level effect sizes conflict between reports. — [Systematic review (PMC6446394)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6446394); [Healio](https://cm.healio.com/news/orthopedics/20140217/more-sleep-may-help-prevent-athletic-injuries-in-adolescents)

### Inferences
- Foam rolling for 30-60 s per area is optional within a warm-up, mainly for a short-term ROM boost. It does not earn a place over balance or calf work.
- Sleep is likely the highest-value recovery behaviour, but the injury data come from adolescents.

### Gaps
- Not researched within the call budget: cold-water immersion, compression, massage guns, and masters-specific recovery timelines (e.g. whether 40-year-olds need longer between hard sessions).
- No evidence specific to masters basketball players on recovery was retrieved.
