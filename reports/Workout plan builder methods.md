# Coaches build volume; the evidence backs logging

Coaches and the evidence point the same way for the plan generator. Pick the split by days available, train each muscle about twice a week, count indirect sets at half weight, put the priority lift first, and progress mostly by **changing exercises in planned blocks and slowly adding sets**. Small fixed rep bumps and calendar deloads are a weaker fit. The user's coach programs fit that pattern: exercises change about every **2 weeks**, total weekly sets rise **about 1.1x** over a fat-loss or durability program (**1.6x** in the PJF band program), reps rise in only **0-26%** of week-to-week repeats, and **none of the 15 programs has a deload week** ([LEARNED_PATTERNS](../docs/LEARNED_PATTERNS.md)). The research supports moderate volume with diminishing returns, hard sets for muscle, rest of at least 60-90 s, double progression and autoregulation from logged sets. It says almost nothing about warm-up rounds, where core goes in the session, or deload timing. On those points coach practice is the best guide available, and it is thin. My verdict on the six findings: **all six are "adopt with changes"**. Rest and progression rest on moderate evidence; core volume, core placement, warm-ups and deloads rest on weak evidence or judgment. The biggest structural change is to make `volume_targets` and rest **goal-specific** instead of global. The adaptive layer should then take over the one job a static template does badly, which is progressing load and reps from what the user actually logged. One limitation applies throughout: the research team's network blocked almost every official page (builtwithscience.com, pjfperformance.com, marketplace.trainheroic.com, athleanx.com, fitbod.me, help.rpstrength.com, sportrxiv.org). The coach and app findings below therefore rest on search-engine summaries of those pages, often second-hand, and not on full reads. The fat-loss sample is also a single PJF program in three phases, so "the coaches" means one coach for that goal.

## Four coaches, one skeleton, different progression engines

On split choice the coaches mostly agree, and the app's current split table already matches them. Ethier recommends full body 3x/week as the best result per hour, upper/lower at 4 days and PPL at 5-6. His reason is that, with volume matched, training a muscle twice a week grows it more than once a week ([BWS best split](https://builtwithscience.com/workouts/best-workout-split/); [BWS full body](https://builtwithscience.com/workouts/full-body-workout-routine/)). Nippard sells the same program at several frequencies. His 5-day Min-Max adds an Arms day to an Upper/Lower/Push/Pull base, so the extra day goes to small muscles rather than another full repeat of the split ([Min-Max Phase 2](https://jeffnippard.com/products/min-max-program-phase-2)). Athlean-X tells beginners to do 2-3 full-body days and uses PPL or upper/lower later ([Athlean-X split article](https://learn.athleanx.com/articles/the-best-way-to-split-up-your-workouts-surprise)). Its signature "train like an athlete" structure keeps conditioning separate from lifting. AX-1, for example, runs **3 strength days plus 2 conditioning days** ([LinLog review](https://linlog.skepticats.com/entries/2020/12/athlean-x-review.php)). PJF splits the week by day type: Vert Code Elite reviews describe separate jump days and mobility/core days ([9to5strength](https://9to5strength.com/vert-code-elite-full-review/)). Griffith writes full-body sessions with one focus per day: posterior vs anterior chain, groin, hip mobility, or pelvis-spine ([TrainHeroic Runners](https://marketplace.trainheroic.com/workout-plan/program/griffith-program-1755617891)).

On volume and effort, the Built With Science camp has moved to **fewer, harder sets**. Nippard's Min-Max uses **1-2 hard working sets per exercise at 0-1 RIR** ([Min-Max Phase 2](https://jeffnippard.com/products/min-max-program-phase-2)). He cites the finding that even 1-4 sets per muscle per week produce measurable growth ([Essentials](https://jeffnippard.com/products/the-essentials-program)). Ethier progresses by double progression: 3x8-12, add reps until the top of the range, then add 10 lb and drop back to 8 ([BWS progressive overload](https://builtwithscience.com/fitness-tips/progressive-overload-techniques/)). Nippard keeps exercises fixed for **four weeks** before swapping them ([Essentials](https://jeffnippard.com/products/the-essentials-program)). He puts deloads at block boundaries, using an intro week 1 and a deload that opens Block 2 ([Nippard PPL](https://jeffnippard.com/products/push-pull-legs-hypertrophy-program)). Ethier suggests a deload every **4-8 weeks** or when the lifter feels run down, and admits there is little formal research behind it ([BWS deload](https://builtwithscience.com/fitness-tips/deload-week/)). BWS's article recommends **at least 3 min of rest on heavy compounds and about 2 min on isolations** ([BWS rest](https://builtwithscience.com/workouts/rest-between-sets/)). Its own app timer reportedly defaults much shorter ([BWS support](https://builtwithscience-support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/39351660348571-How-does-the-rest-timer-work)). Nippard ramps up only on the primary lifts: **3-4+ ramp sets for heavy (1-7 rep) work and 2-3 for lighter work**, with reps falling as load rises ([Fundamentals PDF copy](https://kevindelaplante.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/JeffNippardsFundamentalsHypertrophyProgram.pdf)). For abs he uses about 2 exercises x 3 sets, twice a week ([Fitness Volt](https://fitnessvolt.com/jeff-nippard-6-pack-abs-tips/)).

Athlean-X and PJF progress by **phase change, not week-to-week arithmetic**. Athlean-X programs are typically three 4-week phases. Each phase changes exercises and adds techniques such as supersets, drop sets and 10x10 sets ([Max Size](https://athleanx.com/max-size); [Fitbod Max Size review](https://fitbod.me/blog/athlean-x-max-size-program-review/)). Fat-loss programs such as Max Shred move from conditioning-heavy to lifting-heavy across the phases ([Workout Clarity](https://workoutclarity.com/athlean-x-max-shred-review-my-opinion/)). Cavaliere's stated rules include no failure on the first exercise, face pulls after every workout, and core trained both inside every lift and directly as a finisher ([face pulls](https://learn.athleanx.com/articles/shoulders-for-men/do-face-pulls-after-every-workout); [abs in every exercise](https://learn.athleanx.com/articles/abs-for-men/six-pack-trick-incorporate-abs-in-every-exercise)). On warm-ups he takes the contrarian view that they are usually **too long, not too short** ([Athlean-X warm-up](https://learn.athleanx.com/articles/are-you-screwing-up-your-warmup-big-workout-mistake)). PJF's public program structure is a sequence of phases: Vert Code runs **2/2/4/4-week phases** (Build the Base, Absorb & Redirect, Explode, Spring) ([A1Athlete](https://a1athlete.com/vert-code-bodyweight-review/)), and Durability Code Elite is **8 weeks Restore then 8 weeks Prime** ([PJF online](https://online.pjfperformance.net/groups/the-durability-code-elite/)). No review mentions a PJF deload. Griffith's 6-8 week TrainHeroic blocks "vary loading strategies and movement patterns" ([Dynamic Strength](https://marketplace.trainheroic.com/workout-plan/program/griffith-program-1705431456)). His programs progress by adding load, isometrics or dynamic variants to a mobility pattern rather than adding reps ([Advanced Mobility Explored](https://marketplace.trainheroic.com/workout-plan/program/griffith-program-1610928375)).

| Dimension | BWS / Nippard | Athlean-X | PJF | Griffith |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Split | FB 3x, U/L 4x, PPL or hybrid 5-6x | FB for beginners; strength days plus separate conditioning days | Day types (jump, weight room, mobility/core) | Full body, one focus per day |
| Volume and effort | Few hard sets, 0-1 RIR on isolations | Conventional 6-12 reps, 1-2 RIR; techniques per phase | Not public (user's PDFs: ~3 sets/exercise) | Not public |
| Progression | Double progression; exercises fixed ~4 wk | New exercises and technique every 4-wk phase | New quality and exercises each 2-8 wk phase | Add load, isometrics or variants |
| Deload | Every 4-8 wk, or an intro week | Challenge week (wiki, unverified) | None found | None found |
| Warm-up | Ramps on primary lifts only | Short, specific | Not public (user's PDFs: 2-3 rounds) | Not public (user's PDFs: 2 rounds) |

Much of the PJF and Griffith detail the user asked about is **not published anywhere the researchers could reach**: within-session order, isometric hold times, plyometric contacts, warm-up rounds and fat-loss rest. A search for "Fat Don't Fly" returned nothing, although the program exists in the user's app. For these two coaches, the user's own program files are the primary source, and they are better data than any public page.

## Apps either fill a fixed template or recompute daily from recovery

The app builders split into two designs. Fitbod is the only one with a documented **per-muscle recovery model**. It turns logged sets, reps and load into a 0-100% recovery score per muscle and treats a muscle as fully recovered after about **6-7 days** ([Fitbod help: recovery](https://help.fitbod.me/hc/en-us/articles/360006269014-Muscle-Recovery)). It then chooses each day's exercises according to which muscles are fresh. Its load comes from an estimated "theoretical 1RM", and it learns preferences from skips, swaps and "never again" flags ([Fitbod help: how it creates workouts](https://help.fitbod.me/hc/en-us/articles/360004429814-How-Fitbod-Creates-Your-Workout)). RP Hypertrophy, Juggernaut AI, Alpha Progression and Hevy Trainer instead generate a **fixed multi-week template and adjust the numbers inside it**. Hevy raises load only after the user hits the top of the rep range **on all prescribed sets** ([Hevy help](https://help.hevyapp.com/hc/en-us/articles/38385724273047-Hevy-Trainer-Explained-How-It-Builds-Your-Workout-Program)). Alpha Progression prescribes weight, reps and RIR per set from the last performance. When the next plate jump is too large, it raises load and lowers the rep target together ([AP weight progression](https://alphaprogression.com/en/blog/weight-progression-muscle-and-strength-gain)). MacroFactor does the same in the other direction, suggesting a lighter weight with more reps ([MacroFactor help](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/305-understanding-and-using-smart-progressions)). Juggernaut AI adjusts weight and volume set to set from entered RPE and revises the next week from performance plus a readiness check-in. It tells users to **under-rate effort** so that the changes stay gradual ([Juggernaut RPE help](https://help.jtsstrength.com/en/articles/2-all-about-rpe-and-rir); [individualization](https://help.jtsstrength.com/en/articles/3-how-juggernautai-is-individualized-to-you)).

Volume and deload logic is where the apps differ most. After each session RP asks the user to rate pump, soreness/recovery and workload. Low pump, barely any soreness and an easy workload add sets; strong pump, normal healing and a challenging workload hold volume. The app ramps volume through the mesocycle and **always makes the last week a deload** ([RP help: sets](https://help.rpstrength.com/hc/en-us/articles/32600173777815-How-does-the-app-determine-when-to-add-weight-reps-and-sets); [RP help: deloads](https://help.rpstrength.com/hc/en-us/articles/33510413024279-Does-the-app-automatically-place-deloads)). Dr. Muscle uses a trigger instead: when estimated 1RM falls it schedules a deload that cuts sets by **about 50%**, and the user can cancel it ([Dr. Muscle deload](https://dr-muscle.com/deload/)). Future and Caliber replace the algorithm with a human coach who revises the program weekly from logged data. What a coach adds is context and accountability, not better load maths ([SensAI Future review](https://www.sensai.fit/blog/future-app-review-2026)). The user complaints are the most useful design input. Fitbod users report loads far too heavy (50 kg rows suggested for a 20 kg lifter) or stubbornly too low, recommendations that **ignore manual overrides**, and workouts that feel random ([Appshunter reviews](https://appshunter.io/ios/app/1041517543/reviews/2); [Justuseapp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/1041517543/fitbod-gym-fitness-planner/reviews)). No vendor publishes its formulas, increments or thresholds. Every number below that is attributed to an "app approach" is a design choice in the spirit of that app, not a copy of it.

## Research firmly backs volume, effort and logging, and little else

The strongest evidence concerns volume. Pelland et al. pooled **67 studies (~2,058 participants)**. Weekly volume raised both hypertrophy and strength, with returns diminishing sooner for strength. Counting an indirect set as **0.5** predicted outcomes best. Once volume is matched, frequency barely affects hypertrophy but does help strength ([Sponet record of Pelland et al., Sports Med 2026](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)). Schoenfeld's 2017 meta-analysis found a graded volume effect but could not show a benefit above 10 sets ([Schoenfeld 2017](https://researchgate.net/profile/Brad-Schoenfeld/publication/305455324_Dose-response_relationship_between_weekly_resistance_training_volume_and_increases_in_muscle_mass_A_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis/links/59dc0269458515e9ab4527d6/Dose-response-relationship-between-weekly-resistance-training-volume-and-increases-in-muscle-mass-A-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis.pdf)). The app's counting method (full sets for the main muscle, half for up to three others) already matches the best-supported scheme. Proximity to failure matters for muscle but much less for strength: hypertrophy improves as sets end closer to failure, while strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR ([FAU on Robinson et al. 2024](https://www.fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/muscle-growth-strength-study)). Autoregulation is **at least as good as fixed loading**. In athletes it was better (ES 0.64) ([Zhang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.651112)); in trained adults it was equal ([Hickmott et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35038063/)). That is enough to justify an adaptive layer driven by logs.

For rest, a Bayesian meta-analysis found a **small hypertrophy benefit for rest longer than 60 s and no appreciable gain beyond about 90 s** ([Singer et al. 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/abstract)). The researchers found no data showing that short rest burns meaningfully more fat. For progression, adding load and adding reps gave similar results in trained lifters, with load slightly ahead for 1RM ([Plotkin et al. 2022](https://peerj.com/articles/14142)). Planned exercise variation helps regional hypertrophy and strength, while excessive random rotation can hurt ([Kassiano et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/)). Deload evidence is thin. The one trial found that a full one-week training stop at week 5 of 9 **left hypertrophy unchanged and slightly reduced strength gains** ([Coleman et al. 2024](https://peerj.com/articles/16777)). A Delphi study of expert coaches calls deloading widely used but poorly researched ([Bell et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10511399/)). For exercise order, strength improves most in whatever lift comes first, and order does not affect hypertrophy ([Nunes et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32077380/)).

Several findings bear on specific goals. For durability, the Copenhagen adductor programme cut groin problems by **41%** with a ready-made dose: **3x/week for 6-8 weeks, then 1x/week** ([Harøy et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6851742)). Nordic-hamstring programmes roughly halved hamstring injuries in one meta-analysis (RR 0.49) ([van Dyk 2019 summary](https://www.physio-network.com/research-reviews/thigh/including-the-nordic-hamstring-exercise-in-injury-prevention-programmes-halves-the-rate-of-hamstring-injuries-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-8459-athletes/)), although a reappraisal of the RCTs called that result inconclusive ([reappraisal](https://physio-network.com/research-reviews/thigh/why-methods-matter-in-a-meta-analysis-a-reappraisal-showed-inconclusive-injury-preventive-effect-of-nordic-hamstring-exercise)). For athletic work, vertical jump gains were most likely with **more than 10 weeks, more than 20 sessions and more than 50 jumps per session** ([de Villarreal 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19197203/)). Concurrent aerobic training does not hurt hypertrophy or maximal strength but does blunt explosive strength (SMD -0.28), mostly when done in the same session ([Schumann et al. 2022](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/71546/1/Schumann2021_Article_CompatibilityOfConcurrentAerob.pdf)). That supports AX-style separate conditioning days for the athletic goal. The researchers retrieved **no evidence at all** on core or glute dose, core placement, or how many warm-up rounds to do. Those three verdicts below therefore rest on coach practice and judgment.

## Six measured findings: all adopt with changes, two with solid evidence

| # | Finding | Verdict | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | More core and glute volume for fat loss and durability | Adopt with changes: goal-specific bands, scaled to days per week | Weak (coach practice; indirect volume evidence) |
| 2 | Core mid-session | Adopt with changes: low-fatigue core early only for fat loss, durability and athletic | Weak (judgment plus order specificity) |
| 3 | 2-3 warm-up rounds | Adopt with changes: 2 by default, 3 for fat loss in 45+ min sessions, ramp sets separate | Very weak (coach practice only) |
| 4 | ~60 s rest on main lifts for fat loss | Adopt with changes: 90 s on main lifts, 60 s elsewhere and in supersets | Moderate (Singer 2024) |
| 5 | Progress by volume and exercise change, rarely reps | Adopt with changes: keep 2-week accessory rotation, add a ~10-20% set ramp, move rep/load progression to logs | Moderate for levers; weak for ramp size |
| 6 | No deload weeks | Adopt with changes: drop the automatic week-6 deload, use a triggered one | Weak (Coleman 2024, Delphi) |

**Finding 1, core and glute volume.** The gap is large and consistent within each goal. Abs run 11-12 sets/week and obliques 9-17 in the fat-loss and durability programs, against a rule of 2-6. Glutes run **22-23 against 9-15**. The BWS muscle programs, by contrast, sit at abs 2 and obliques 1, which is inside the current rule ([LEARNED_PATTERNS](../docs/LEARNED_PATTERNS.md)). The coaches are therefore not doing something the rule missed for every goal; they are doing something goal-specific. The volume literature supports more sets with diminishing returns and applies half-set counting to glutes worked by squats and hinges ([Pelland via Sponet](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)). It has no core-specific dose. Two caveats limit how far to copy the coach numbers. The fat-loss figures come from a **6-day** program, so 23 glute sets over 3 days would mean about 8 per session. Durability core is largely isometric, anti-rotation and carry work, which costs little recovery. Adopt goal-specific bands as follows. Fat loss: abs 8-12, obliques 6-10, glutes 12-20. Durability: abs 8-12, obliques 10-16, glutes 15-22, adductors 4-8, which also accommodates the Copenhagen dose. Athletic: abs 4-8, obliques 4-8, glutes 12-18. Keep muscle and strength at abs 2-6 and obliques 0-6. Scale the upper bound by `days/6` when the user trains fewer than 5 days. The bands and the scaling are judgment.

**Finding 2, core mid-session.** PJF puts core at position **0.3** in fat-loss sessions (main lifts at 0.4) and 0.5 in durability sessions. BWS puts it at **1.0**, the end of the session, and Cavaliere treats direct ab work as a finisher ([LEARNED_PATTERNS](../docs/LEARNED_PATTERNS.md); [Athlean-X abs](https://learn.athleanx.com/articles/abs-for-men/the-perfect-abs-workout)). No retrieved study tests core pre-fatigue. Order research says only that whatever comes first improves most ([Nunes 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32077380/)), which argues against tiring the trunk before a heavy squat or hinge. Moderate-load fat-loss sessions at 8 reps carry little of that risk. The coaches also pair core with non-competing lifts, as in the observed anti-extension + horizontal pull and hinge + lateral flexion supersets. Add a core `placement` field. Set it to `"early"` for fat_loss, durability and athletic, with low-fatigue patterns only (anti-extension, anti-rotation, isometric, lateral flexion), 2-3 sets, far from failure, placed after power work or supersetted with secondary lifts. Set it to `"end"` for strength and muscle, and for any loaded or to-failure core work.

**Finding 3, warm-up rounds.** The coaches use 2 rounds (athletic, durability) and 3 rounds (fat loss), against a rule of 1. At the current `secs_per_set` of 40 with 4 exercises, 1 round takes about 2.7 min, 2 rounds about 5.3 min and 3 rounds 8 min. That fits a 60-min session but crowds a 30-min one. Nippard's 2-4 ramp sets on primary lifts point the same way ([Fundamentals PDF copy](https://kevindelaplante.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/JeffNippardsFundamentalsHypertrophyProgram.pdf)). Cavaliere's warning against long, unfocused warm-ups pushes back ([Athlean-X warm-up](https://learn.athleanx.com/articles/are-you-screwing-up-your-warmup-big-workout-mistake)), and no warm-up research was retrieved. Set general warm-up rounds per session length: 1-2 at 30 min, 2 at 45 min, 2 at 60 min (3 for fat_loss). Generate ramp sets separately, for the first main lift only: about 40-50% x 8, 60-70% x 4-5 and 80-85% x 2-3. The ramp template is inferred from Nippard's rules, not published by him.

**Finding 4, 60 s rest for fat loss.** This is the finding with the best evidence, and it argues for a compromise. Rest longer than 60 s gives a small hypertrophy edge, and nothing appreciable is gained beyond about 90 s ([Singer 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/abstract)). Keeping muscle is the point of lifting in a deficit, and no retrieved data shows that short rest burns more fat. The current 150 s, however, is longer than the evidence requires for 8-10 rep work. Set fat_loss main lifts to **90 s**, and secondary and accessory work to **60 s**, with antagonist supersets carrying the density, as the coaches already do with push/pull pairs. Over a session this saves about 3 min per main lift, which roughly pays for the extra warm-up round and core sets from findings 1 and 3. Keep 2-3 min for strength-goal main lifts, where BWS and the volume-load mechanism both argue for longer rest ([BWS rest](https://builtwithscience.com/workouts/rest-between-sets/)).

**Finding 5, progression levers.** Part of this finding is already in the rule: secondary and accessory picks rotate every 2 weeks, and the coaches' median of 2 weeks matches it ([LEARNED_PATTERNS](../docs/LEARNED_PATTERNS.md)). The real disagreements are the fixed **+1 rep in week 2** and the absence of a set ramp. Load and rep progression work about equally well ([Plotkin 2022](https://peerj.com/articles/14142)), so neither lever is wrong. A blind +1 rep ignores what the user actually did, though, which is exactly what the adaptive layer should decide. Volume is the lever with the clearest dose-response ([Pelland via Sponet](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)). Change the rule to: keep main lifts for **4-6 weeks** (Nippard's four-week rule and Kassiano's warning against random rotation), and rotate accessories every 2 weeks. At each phase change, add 1 set to 1-2 exercises per day so that total weekly sets reach about **1.1x by the end for fat loss and durability and up to 1.2x for muscle and athletic**. Make the +1 rep (and +5 s hold) a fallback used only when no logs exist. Do not generalize the band program's 1.6x: low-load band sets cost far less than barbell sets.

**Finding 6, deloads.** None of the 15 coach programs deloads, including the 8-week Durability Code Prime, whose weekly sets climb from 152 to 185 ([LEARNED_PATTERNS](../docs/LEARNED_PATTERNS.md)). The evidence shows no hypertrophy cost from skipping a deload and a small strength cost from a full stop ([Coleman 2024](https://peerj.com/articles/16777)). A half-sets final week of a 6-week plan also has an odd effect: the plan ends on its easiest week. Remove the automatic deload. Replace it with the adaptive trigger described below. Keep an optional scheduled deload for strength and muscle plans of **8+ weeks**, placed as week 1 of the *next* block in Nippard's intro-week style rather than as the plan's finale ([Nippard PPL](https://jeffnippard.com/products/push-pull-legs-hypertrophy-program)).

## Ranked rule changes and an adaptive layer driven by logs

The changes below are ordered by impact on the user divided by implementation cost. All keys refer to `data/design_rules.json`. The human copy in `docs/PROGRAM_DESIGN.md` needs the same edits, and the `_doc` field says to keep the two in sync.

| Priority | Change in `design_rules.json` | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Add `goals.<goal>.volume_overrides` for abs, obliques, glutes and adductors (bands in finding 1), scaled by days/week | Coach data; Pelland counting. Judgment for the bands |
| 2 | Add `goals.<goal>.rest_secs` per role: fat_loss main 90, secondary/accessory 60; strength main 150-180 | Singer 2024 (moderate) |
| 3 | `phases.deload`: replace "last week of a 6+ week plan" with `"trigger": "adaptive"`, plus an optional scheduled intro deload for strength/muscle plans of 8+ weeks | Coach data; Coleman 2024 (weak) |
| 4 | `phases`: add `main_keep_weeks: 4` and a set ramp (`sets_add_per_phase: 1` on 1-2 exercises/day, `max_total_factor` 1.1-1.2 by goal); demote `week2.reps_add` to a no-log fallback | Plotkin, Kassiano, Pelland (moderate); ramp size judgment |
| 5 | `roles.warmup.rounds` per session length (30: 1-2, 45: 2, 60: 2, fat_loss 60: 3), plus a separate ramp-set generator for the first main lift | Coach practice (very weak) |
| 6 | `roles.core`: `sets: 3` for fat_loss and durability; add `placement` (`early` for fat_loss/durability/athletic with low-fatigue patterns only, `end` otherwise) | Coach practice; order specificity (weak) |
| 7 | Durability: Copenhagen adduction 3x/week in weeks 1-6, then 1x/week; one Nordic or eccentric hamstring slot per week | Harøy 2019 (strong); van Dyk 2019 (contested) |
| 8 | Athletic: power `sets: 2` (coach median), 40-100 contacts/session, plyometrics before lifting, hard conditioning on separate days or ≥3 h apart | de Villarreal 2009, Schumann 2022 (moderate); contact range judgment |

The adaptive layer should copy the double-progression family of Hevy and Alpha Progression, as the cheapest well-proven design. Borrow Dr. Muscle's e1RM trigger for deloads, and Fitbod's lesson that a user's override must win.

| Component | Rule | Evidence-backed or judgment |
|---|---|---|
| Inputs | Per set: weight, reps (or hold seconds), date, exercise id with fractional muscle tags; optional 1-tap effort rating (easy / right / hard); plan rep range and target RIR; skips and swaps | Inputs mirror Hevy, AP and Juggernaut; RIR use backed by autoregulation meta-analyses |
| Per-session load/rep rule | If every working set hit the top of the range at target effort, raise load by the smallest increment (2.5 kg barbell, next dumbbell) and reset reps to the bottom. If the increment exceeds ~10% of load, pick the rep target that keeps Epley e1RM roughly constant instead. If reps fall below the range bottom in 2 consecutive sessions, cut load 5-10%. Holds: +5 s until the top of the range, then a harder variation | Double progression: Plotkin, Hevy, AP. The 10%, 5-10% and 2-session thresholds are judgment |
| Overrides and new exercises | A manual load change becomes the new baseline immediately. A swapped-in or new exercise starts conservatively (estimated RIR ≥3) and is capped relative to related logged lifts. Show the reason ("12/12/12 last time → +2.5 kg") | Judgment drawn from Fitbod complaints |
| Volume rule | Per muscle and week, sum fractional sets. At each phase change add 1 set (within the goal band and the `max_total_factor`) only if that muscle's e1RM or reps rose over the phase and adherence was ≥80%. Hold if flat; remove 1 set if the user rated sessions "hard" twice in a row. Optional RP-style pump/soreness/workload survey can replace the performance check | Volume dose-response (Pelland); add/hold/remove logic from RP. Thresholds are judgment |
| Deload trigger | Trigger when e1RM falls on ≥2 main lifts across 2 consecutive exposures, or reps miss the range on ≥3 exercises in one week, or the user asks. ## Conclusion

The coach data refutes two parts of the current generator, the global volume table and the calendar deload. Above all, it shows that the generator is trying to be both a program designer and a progression engine. The coaches' programs are almost purely designs: they change exercises, add a few sets and never touch reps, because a PDF cannot see the lifter. Apps exist to supply the missing half. So the generator should copy what coaches do well (goal-specific volume, block-wise exercise rotation, more prep and core for fat loss and durability), and the adaptive layer should own everything the logs can answer (load, reps, when to add sets, when to back off). The fixed "+1 rep in week 2" and "half sets in week 6" are the two rules that each layer should hand off to the other.

The weakest links are worth stating, because they mark what to check next. The fat-loss conclusions rest on one coach's single program. Core dose, core placement and warm-up rounds have no research behind them. Every vendor threshold is undisclosed. Every coach and app claim above comes from search summaries of pages the researchers could not open. The cheapest way to strengthen this is the user's own data. Once the adaptive layer has logged a few blocks, the user's e1RM trends can decide whether the higher core and glute volume, and the absence of deloads, actually work for them. That evidence would beat anything this report could cite.
